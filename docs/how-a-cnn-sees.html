How a CNN sees a picture

One tiny example, step by step: a 7×7 picture of the letter L, two simple filters, and the feature maps they make. Your ResNet-18 does exactly this, just bigger.

Step 1 · The picture

Each square is one pixel. 1 means ink, an empty square means 0 (background). Real CIFAR images are 32×32 with 3 colours, but the idea is the same.

Multiply each pixel by the filter weight on top of it, then add the 9 results. A high number means "this spot looks like my pattern". On an empty spot the answer is 0.

Then subtract a small threshold (1) and turn negatives into 0. That's the ReLU step: weak matches disappear, strong ones stay. So this spot gets 3 − 1 = 2.

Now slide the filter one square to the right, do it again, and repeat for every position. Writing all the answers into a new grid gives a feature map.

Same picture, two filters, two maps. Map 1 lights up along the upright stroke of the L; map 2 lights up along the bottom stroke. Everything else is 0.

This is what a feature map is: a grid the same size as the picture, where each number says how strongly one pattern appears at that spot. The first layer of your ResNet makes 64 of these (64 filters), for edges and colours.
Step 5 · The next layer

This layer's filter asks: "is there a vertical line just above me AND a horizontal line just to my right?" It reads both maps at once and adds the two answers (then the same threshold and ReLU).

Only one spot says yes: the bottom-left corner of the L. So this new feature map is a corner detector, built from the two line detectors.

The key idea: every layer does the same thing as step 3, but its "picture" is the previous layer's feature maps. Lines → corners → shapes → parts → objects. Each layer builds on the one before.
Step 6 · Summarise

Average each map into one number

Feature mapAverageMeaning
vertical lines0.16some vertical line somewhere
horizontal lines0.16some horizontal line somewhere
corners0.04a corner somewhere

This is the average pooling step. It forgets where things were and keeps how much of each pattern the picture has. The result is a short list of numbers: the feature vector. In your ResNet it has 512 numbers.

Step 7 · The head decides

The head turns the feature vector into an answer

Possible answerWhat the head checksScore
Lvertical + horizontal + a cornerhigh ✓
Ivertical, but no horizontallow
a flat linehorizontal, but no verticallow

The head gives each possible answer a score from the feature vector; the highest wins. Everything before the head is the backbone (it understands the picture); the head only makes the final choice. That's why your experiment can swap heads between datasets and keep the backbone.
